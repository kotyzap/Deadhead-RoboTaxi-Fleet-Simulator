dead·head
noun, and also verb
1Taxi trade. To drive with nobody in the back. The miles between dropping one passenger and reaching the next — real distance, real power, real wear, and not one cent of fare against any of it.
2The other reading. A man whose work has been automated out from under him. Still moving. Still costing something to keep on the road. No longer carrying anybody anywhere.

The game is named for both, and it means both. Deadhead miles are a live number on your books — the single figure that decides whether a shift made money — and they are also, fairly bluntly, the thing that happened to you before the game started.

The story

Three acts, and you can fail the middle one

Not a scenario list with a difficulty slider. There is a prologue, a five-city stretch that quietly grades you, an application you can be turned down for, and one ending.

Act one · Austin

Your father gave a man your number

Paolo Cortez drove a cab for forty-one years and got out while getting out was still his own idea. He co-owns a dealership now, he is recruiting for an operations centre in San Francisco, and he is not pleased about what he is about to talk you into. He calls you first because your father is a good reference.

You already meet one of the requirements: you own a car that drives itself. Austin is where you find out what that is worth. He walks you through the first shift — which offers to take, why the empty miles matter more than the meter — then goes quiet and lets you get it wrong.

“You're not driving. You're deciding. Turns out that's the part that was hard.”Paolo, somewhere in the first hour
Act two · Five cities

The job is going fine. That's the trap.

Austin, Dallas, Miami, Tampa, Orlando. Each one opens when you clock off a full shift in the last. There is one bank across all five, not a balance per city — so a car you left parked in Miami is still billing you at midnight while you work Tampa, and it will keep doing that until you notice.

Underneath all of it, nine requirements are being counted without being announced. Miles supervised. Shifts completed. Acceptance rate. You are being assessed for something you have not applied for yet.

Apply too early and the same mailbox writes back with an honest “you meet 3 of 9” and tells you which six — not a locked door and no explanation.

Act three · San Francisco

You qualified. Now you're in the seat.

The role you applied for is supervising driver, and it is not the job you have been doing. One car. No fleet. No fast-forward — San Francisco runs at 1× because you are physically in it, and there is nobody to delegate to. The city you have been working toward is the one where you personally have to sit there.

Clear it and the game ends on a signed contract. Not a score, not a win screen, not a leaderboard position — a document, and Paolo in the room, who still thinks you made a mistake.

“I still think it's a step down. But I drove out here to watch you sign it, and I'd do it again.”Paolo, the last thing he says

He never comes around, and the game never argues him out of it. That question is left with you.

New York
The other character

Paolo Cortez

Forty-one years in a cab before he got out, while getting out was still his idea. He walks you through your first day, then goes quiet — and speaks again only once you've been stuck long enough to need it. He isn't a tooltip. He has opinions about what you're doing, and he's usually right.

He turns up in every city you unlock, and he knows what the trade there used to be.

A standing Now line always names the single most useful next thing, so you're never lost. It never tells you what to choose.

● Now: clock on and take the best offer

Forty-one years of it, here. New York froze the number of cabs at 13,595 in 1937 and never really unfroze it — and a medallion that changed hands for $1.3 million in 2014 was worth about $150,000 five years later.

He has one opinion about the whole job, and he never drops it — see Act three ↑

Paolo’s first message is waiting. Nobody makes you accept it — the prologue lets you decline, and means it.

Real data, not set dressing

The numbers are the difficulty setting

Everything around the hardware — Hitchr, Zipp, Meridian, Halo — is invented. The cars and the numbers are not. That line is deliberate.

✓Six real geofences, each drawn from the actual published service area — not a symmetric shape dropped on a map.
✓Real charging sites with actual coordinates, stall counts and peak kW.
✓Real time-of-use electricity from six real utilities, hour by hour.
✓Real MSRP on all six cars, with a cost side scaled to match.
✓Real fares, calibrated against observed prices in each market.
✓Live weather for whichever city you're in.
✓Real road geometry — cars follow actual streets on the map.
✓Where reality made it worse, it was left worse. Miami has no airport zone because the real box doesn't reach one.
